Electrician hourly rate in Australia: how to set yours

Residential service, light commercial, test & tag, data and comms: electricians have narrower margins than most trades once licence fees, test gear and van stock are counted properly. Here is how to work out a rate that pays, with an example a sole-trader sparky can plug straight into.

The formula

Minimum hourly rate (ex GST) = (annual business costs + the wage you need) / billable hours per year. Add your target margin on top, then GST on top of that when you quote the customer.

1. Electrician-specific costs sole traders miss

  • Licence and compliance: electrical contractor licence, electrician's licence renewal, restricted-electrical if you hold one, white card, test-and-tag competency, working-at-heights refresher, CPR and LVR refresher every 12 months.
  • Insurance: public liability (usually $20m), professional indemnity if you certify work, tool insurance, income protection. Many states also require a consumer-protection or contract-works policy over a set contract value.
  • Vehicle: ute or van, fuel, rego, CTP and comprehensive, servicing, tyres, finance or depreciation, van fit-out (racking, drawers, inverter).
  • Test gear calibration: insulation and earth continuity tester, RCD tester, clamp meter, multimeter, thermal imager. Annual calibration on the testers you certify with is not optional, and a replacement set every few years is a real cost.
  • Van stock: common cable sizes, GPOs, switches, RCBOs, LED drivers, tails, consumables. A real working stock sits between $4,000 and $10,000 and has to be insured and topped up.
  • Software and admin: quoting and invoicing, scheduling, accounting, phone and data, website and ads, accountant and bank fees, electrical defect management or CoES/CES/eCoES submission fees in your state.

For a one-van residential sparky this typically lands between $38,000 and $55,000 a year before you pay yourself, with test & tag or data-focused operators sitting in a lower range and anyone doing switchboard or solar work sitting higher.

2. The wage you need, super and leave included

As a sole trader there is no employer putting super aside, paying your sick days or holidays, and nobody else doing admin on your behalf. Build all of that into the wage figure, along with income tax. The gross wage figure a sole-trader electrician typically needs to match a PAYG salary of $95,000 is closer to $118,000 once super, leave loading and admin time are added.

3. Billable hours, not hours worked

Driving between jobs, picking up gear from the wholesaler, quoting, invoicing, follow-ups, CoES paperwork and call-backs all come out of the chargeable day. Most sole-trader electricians bill 55 to 65% of the hours they work. At 46 weeks x 45 hours x 60%, that is about 1,242 billable hours a year.

4. Worked example

LineFigure
Annual business costs (van, insurance, licence, test gear, stock top-up, software, admin)$46,000
Wage you need (incl. super, leave, tax provision)$118,000
Total to earn$164,000
Billable hours (46 x 45 x 60%)1,242
Break-even rate$132/hr ex GST
Add 20% margin for slow months, van replacement and growth$132 / 0.80 = $165/hr ex GST
Quote the customer (GST on top)$181.50/hr incl. GST

Change any one of those numbers and the answer moves. The point is not the final figure; it is that a sparky whose costs and utilisation look like that quietly loses money every hour they charge under $132 ex GST, however busy they are.

5. GST, CoES and call-out fees

If you are registered for GST, work out and track your hourly rate ex GST and add 10% when you quote. Treating a GST-inclusive figure as the rate quietly costs you about a tenth of your income. Use the GST estimate for a single BAS period as a rough check, not a lodged BAS. Compliance submissions (CoES/CES/eCoES in Vic, CCEW in NSW, equivalents elsewhere) are a billable line, not a freebie. A travel or call-out charge recovers the hour you spent driving that you can't bill as electrical work (see call-out fees and travel charges).

Common mistakes sole-trader sparkies make

  • Copying another sparky's rate. Their van, insurance, test gear calibration and stock holding are not yours. Their utilisation almost certainly is not either.
  • Not charging for compliance paperwork. CoES and defect notices take real minutes. They belong on the invoice as a line item or built into the rate.
  • Thin markup on materials. A $1.80 RCBO costs more to source, stock and warrant than the dollar you make on it. See the parts markup guide.
  • Ignoring call-back time. An hour returning to re-tighten a loose neutral is an hour you can't invoice. Track call-back hours and add them to your utilisation calculation.
  • Quoting hours too tight. If a switchboard upgrade keeps taking 6 hours and you quote 4, your effective rate is a third lower than the number on the page.
